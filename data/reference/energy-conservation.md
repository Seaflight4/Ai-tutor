# Work

Work is the transfer of energy by a force acting on an object as it is displaced. For a constant force $\vec{F}$ acting through a displacement $\vec{d}$ at angle $\theta$ between them, the work done is

$$W = Fd \cos\theta.$$

Work is a scalar with units of joules (J); $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}$. Work is positive when the force has a component in the direction of motion (energy added to the object) and negative when opposite (energy removed).

# Kinetic Energy and the Work-Energy Theorem

The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE = \tfrac12 mv^2 - \tfrac12 mv_0^2.$$

Kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2$ is the energy of motion. The work-energy theorem is most useful when the forces are constant or when you care only about the initial and final speeds, not the time taken.

# Potential Energy

Potential energy is stored energy associated with position or configuration. Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface is

$$PE = mgh,$$

where $h$ is the height above a chosen reference level. A spring stores elastic potential energy

$$PE_s = \tfrac12 kx^2,$$

where $k$ is the spring constant and $x$ the displacement from equilibrium. Potential energy belongs to the system (object + Earth, or object + spring), not to the object alone.

# Conservation of Mechanical Energy

When only conservative forces (gravity, springs) do work, the total mechanical energy is conserved:

$$KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f.$$

Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy. This is a powerful shortcut: you can relate initial and final states without solving for forces or time. Choose a reference level for $h$ first, apply the equation to two points, and solve for the unknown. If non-conservative forces like friction act, mechanical energy is not conserved — the lost energy equals the work done by those forces.
