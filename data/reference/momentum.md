# Linear Momentum

Linear momentum is the product of mass and velocity:

$$\vec{p} = m\vec{v}.$$

It is a vector with the same direction as velocity, and SI units of $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$. Momentum is useful because it is conserved under the right conditions, which simplifies collision and explosion problems that would be hard with forces.

# Impulse

Impulse is the change in momentum produced by a force acting over a time interval:

$$\vec{J} = \vec{F}_{\text{net}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \vec{p}.$$

A small force over a long time can produce the same impulse as a large force over a short time. This is why airbags and bent knees reduce injury: they extend $\Delta t$, lowering the peak force for the same $\Delta p$.

# Conservation of Momentum

When the net external force on a system is zero, the total momentum of the system is conserved:

$$\vec{p}_{\text{total},i} = \vec{p}_{\text{total},f}.$$

This is a vector equation — apply it component by component. Internal forces (between objects in the system) cancel by the third law and do not change the total momentum. Collisions and explosions are the classic applications.

# Elastic and Inelastic Collisions

In an elastic collision, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. In an inelastic collision, only momentum is conserved; kinetic energy is lost (to heat, deformation). A perfectly inelastic collision is one where the objects stick together afterward — they share a common final velocity. For two-body 1D collisions, write momentum conservation and (if elastic) kinetic-energy conservation, then solve for the unknown final velocities.
